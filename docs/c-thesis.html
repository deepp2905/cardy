The claim

Ownership doesn't come from possessing an object. It comes from having put something into it.

The IKEA effect: people value what they assembled more than the identical thing bought finished. It's one of the most robustly replicated findings in behavioural economics, and almost nobody in fintech has built anything on it.

Your bank card is the most-touched physical object your bank will ever give you. You carry it every day, you hand it to strangers, you put it on a table in front of people. And it arrives looking exactly like everybody else's, because it was designed for a print run of two million.

So the claim under this project is narrow and testable: if you make someone design their card, they will care about the card. Everything I built is either evidence for that or a constraint protecting it.

The behaviour already exists in the wild

Cash App's payment wand didn't invent anything. It productised something people were already doing: gluing tap-to-pay cards inside homemade wands, and filming themselves paying for things with them.

The first drop of ten thousand units sold out within hours.

That's not a marketing win. That's people hacking a payment object into something expressive because the one they were issued felt dead.

The demand was already proven. Nobody had built the front door — a way to do it that doesn't involve a glue gun.

I found five fintech problems worth solving. Then I asked each one the same question: is there a moment here where a person's own effort could become the thing they value?

A gallery to pick from, or an image to upload. One gives no control, the other gives too much — and neither is customization. But the artefact is permanent, physical, carried daily, and seen by other people.

Why the card wins on the test

It's the only one where a person's effort produces a durable object they carry and other people see. Three of the other four are rate problems in different clothes. The teen wallet was the real contender, and it lost because the thing you make there vanishes on send.

Build

Six decisions, each one an argument

If effort creates ownership, then the design job is narrow: make effort feel good, make it impossible to waste, and give it somewhere to land. Every decision below is one of those three — or it's a decision that turned out to be arguing against the claim, which is why I reversed it.

makes effort
feel good

Nine cards that had to be nine designs

The first version was one design recoloured nine times. If the starting points all rhyme, then choosing between them isn't effort — it's picking a colour, and you know it.

My instinct was to widen the slider ranges. That made it worse: every card just had more room to look wrong. The variety that actually reads is seeded at build time — each colourway gets its own grid angle, mark weight, and how hard the radial wave twists and shoves each cell. Those are the levers that change a pattern's character. The sliders only change density and scale.

Two sliders out of thirteen parameters

The most direct threat to the claim is a user who invests effort and produces something ugly. Then the effort was worse than wasted — it was a lesson in your own bad taste, delivered by a bank.

So the pattern engine's thirteen knobs are pinned to eleven hand-tuned constants, and each of the two exposed sliders maps into a band narrow enough that no position on any track looks like a mistake. Around 54 million distinct cards, and not one of them is ugly.

The constraints are where the actual design work went. If I did that job properly, you never notice they're there at all.

The reachable space comes out at around 54 million distinct cards. That number is not the achievement — anyone can multiply a parameter space out to millions, and most bad customisation tools have a far bigger one. The claim is the second half of the sentence: and not one of them is ugly.

The slider I removed

Phase was a real parameter and it was on the panel. Sliding it moved the wave offset — the ripple slid, and the pattern's character didn't change at all.

This is the sharpest version of the question. If effort creates ownership, does more effort create more of it? No. A control that looks like it does something and does nothing is worse than no control, because it teaches the user their effort doesn't land. It's a constant now.

Every choice I removed made the output better. Fewer knobs on the panel, stronger cards out the other end. Restraint did more here than range ever did.

gives effort
somewhere to land

You have to post it yourself

The card is laid on a carrier sheet, folded in thirds, slid into a kraft envelope, sealed and flipped to show the address side. Then the flow stops and waits — you drag it into the mail slot.

That last gesture is the entire argument in one interaction. A button would have completed the same transaction. Making you perform the posting is what converts five seconds of animation from something you watched into something you did.

It's also impossible to fail. Release anywhere past halfway, or with any downward velocity at all, and it snaps in. The gesture is ceremony, not a test — a flow that can reject your effort at the final step is arguing against itself.

It's yours before you've done anything

Visit /ria-money and the welcome greets Ria and the card already carries RIA MONEY, engraved, before a single choice has been made.

This is the cheapest thing in the build and it might be doing the most work. Ownership needs somewhere to start — and there's a real difference between customising a card and customising your card. The name arrives first so that everything after it is editing rather than creating.

One pure function, read once at mount. No state, no router, no effect — the app never mutates the URL, so the path segment survives the whole journey for free. A charset gate means a scanner probe or /favicon.ico can never be read as a name.

One object, never re-mounted

The card is a single node mounted once that never unmounts across all three steps. Each step reports where it should sit and the card springs to that point.

This is a continuity argument, not a technical one. If the thing you're making disappears and a different-looking thing appears in its place, you've been shown two objects. The whole flow depends on you believing it's the same card the entire way through — the one you made, the one going in the envelope.

It replaced a shared-element hand-off, which popped: the measured box was distorted by the deck's coverflow transform, so the hand-off was reading a lie about where the card was. With one persistent node there's nothing to measure and nothing to hand off.

Honesty

Where the claim didn't help me

A thesis is only useful if it can be wrong about something. Three places it gave me no guidance at all — and one where following it cost me two days.

no guidance

Everything about the carousel

Whether the deck is coverflow or a flat strip or an exploded stack has nothing to do with ownership. I built seven mechanics as grey wireframes and picked by feel, because feel was the only available criterion. Coverflow won. The thesis was silent.

actively cost me

I locked a decision and had to break it

Before writing the ship sequence I wrote a full build spec with a ten-row decision table. D10: fold a snapshot PNG, never the live card — a WebGL canvas inside a 3D transform is a known Safari failure mode.

D10 was defensive against a risk the geometry had already removed. The card sits on the middle panel of a three-panel fold, and the middle panel never rotates. The card also unmounts before anything containing it moves in 3D. The dangerous case couldn't happen.

Reversing it deleted a whole dependency, and the sequence now folds the real card. Writing the spec first is still why the geometry was solid enough to catch it — but a locked decision is only as good as the assumption under it, and mine had expired two phases earlier without anyone re-reading it.

no guidance

The step indicator I rebuilt three times

I built a flexGrow spring, a scaleX wipe, and cumulative fills — and reverted all three to the version I started with. It was better for a reason the thesis has nothing to say about: one object moves, and your eye can track it. Every improvement replaced one travelling fill with three appearing and disappearing.

The bounce I then spent a turn fixing was self-inflicted — my spring was fighting motion's layout projection for the same pixels. It only existed because I'd added it.

no guidance

The overscroll bounce I wrote twice and deleted once

My first resistance curve saturated exponentially and went dead after about 300px of pull — you keep dragging and nothing answers. The right shape is a rational asymptote that approaches its ceiling without arriving.

Then I deleted the whole thing anyway. A custom rubberband runs on top of the platform's native elastic scroll rather than replacing it, and the two fighting is what felt wrong. It came back later, once I'd rebuilt the deck on an index I owned instead of a scroll container.

Underneath

The parts nobody sees

The card is ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1 — 85.6 × 53.98mm, 3.18mm radius — at every size it appears. The whole ship sequence is drawn in real millimetres and converted by a single scale factor. No raw pixel value exists inside the stage except hairlines and shadows.

Sheet → packet → envelope → slot

to scale

Paper doesn't obey the theme

Every colour in the app comes from one 12-step neutral ramp that inverts between light and dark — and the cards are the only source of colour anywhere. Paper and kraft are the sole exceptions to the ramp: paper that changes colour with the UI theme stops reading as a physical object.

The fidelity gap is the focus device

The carrier sheet is a deliberate lo-fi wireframe — placeholder bars, no real copy. The card is the only fully rendered object in frame, so even while it's being covered up it stays the hero.

Slowing seven springs without changing any of them

The sequence runs 1.25× slower than it was first tuned. Not seven re-tunings — slowing by k/T² and c/T holds the damping ratio exactly, so the overshoot and the settle shape are identical and only the pace changes. Which mattered because the envelope's flip is deliberately under-damped, and that small rock as it lands is the signature of the beat.

Original tuning vs shipped tempo · same ζ

Reduced motion, since it's usually a lie

Not a switch that turns animation off. The wrap sequence collapses to two crossfades. The deck becomes a flat draggable strip. The drag-to-post becomes a button — because the terminal action of a flow can never be gesture-only. If the posting gesture is the argument, then a user who can't perform gestures still has to be able to make the argument.

Process

A playground before the product

Carousel behaviour, colour, typography, card graphics and the whole envelope choreography were tested on separate dev routes first, each with its own control panel and a copy button handing back apply-ready values. Nothing landed in the real build before I knew how it would behave.

The ship sequence was built against a scrubbable timeline — you cannot tune the fourth beat of a five-second animation if you have to sit through the first three every time.

Working with AI isn't prompting for output. It's writing the constraints down hard enough that the output has nowhere bad to go.

Did it work?

I can't prove the thesis with a five-day prototype and no users. What I can say is that it was a useful claim to hold: it decided which of five opportunities to take, it justified capping the interface at two sliders when my instinct was to add more, and it's the reason the flow makes you post the envelope yourself instead of pressing a button.

And it correctly identified the one control I should delete. A slider that looks like it does something and does nothing teaches you your effort doesn't land — which is the exact opposite of what the whole thing is for.

AI got me to ninety percent in two days. The last ten percent took the rest of the week, and it's the only part anybody feels when they use it.
