Five problems worth solving

Each of these belongs to a different person, in a different moment, with something different at stake.

01

Currency rate charts

Someone timing a tuition payment gets the same line graph everyone got in 2009. The rate is the whole product for these people, and the chart is still an afterthought.

02

Send-money interaction for teens

Teens transact constantly, but every wallet they touch is really designed for the parent funding it. The interaction is what would make them stay — not the balance.

03

A card that's actually yours

Banks give you a gallery to pick from, or an image to upload. One offers no control and the other offers too much. Neither is customization in any sense that matters.

Chosen
04

Receiving money, but delightful

Money arrives as a push notification and a reference number. The most emotional moment in the transfer is the one nobody has bothered to design.

05

A rate alert setter

Freelancers and expats want to catch a rate, not watch one all week. Setting the threshold should feel as satisfying as the moment it's hit.

Why this one

Three of those five are rate problems in different clothes. I picked the one furthest from a rate table on purpose — it had the most room for motion and feel, and it was the only one with a genuine magic moment already sitting inside it.

And the demand signal was already in the wild. Cash App's payment wand didn't invent a behaviour, it productised one: people were already gluing tap-to-pay cards inside homemade wands and filming themselves paying with them. The first drop of ten thousand sold out in hours. That's people hacking a payment object into something expressive because the one they were issued felt dead.

The IKEA effect, applied to a piece of plastic that otherwise arrives looking like everybody else's.

01of three

Welcome

The shortest screen in the product, and the one carrying the most freight: it has to establish tone, and it has to already know who you are.

Visit /ria-money and the headline greets Ria, and the card behind it already carries RIA MONEY engraved. Before you have made a single choice, before you have touched anything, the object on screen is already yours.

The nine colourways

click to cycle

Nine cards that had to look like nine designs

The first version was one design recoloured nine times, and I could see it immediately. My instinct was to widen the slider ranges — more room to differ. That made it worse: every card just had more room to look wrong.

The variety that actually reads isn't in the sliders. It's seeded at build time: each colourway gets its own grid angle, mark weight, and how hard the radial wave twists, pulses and shoves each cell. Those are the levers that change a pattern's character. The sliders mostly change density and scale.

Shape and fill are dealt round-robin rather than rolled, so the strip always shows three circles, three squares, three triangles and both fill modes. A random draw clumped — four triangles and one square is a plausible roll, and it looks like a bug.

Every card, deterministically

same seed, every visit

Two sliders out of thirteen parameters

The pattern engine has around thirteen knobs. The interface exposes two. Every other one is pinned to a constant I tuned by hand, and each slider maps 0..1 into a band narrow enough that no position on any track looks like a mistake.

That comes out at around 54 million distinct cards — and the number isn't the achievement. Anyone can multiply a parameter space out to millions; the work was making sure every one of those states is worth having.

The constraints are where the actual design work went. If I did that job properly, you never notice they're there at all.

A third slider that didn't earn its place

Phase was on the panel. Sliding it moved the wave offset — the ripple slid, and the pattern's character didn't change at all. It looked like it did something. It did nothing worth a control. It's now a constant, and the URL parameter for it was dropped, so older share links render at zero.

Every choice I removed made the output better. Fewer knobs on the panel, stronger cards out the other end.

The carousel took three attempts

v1

A native scroll container with mandatory snap. It worked and it was not draggable, and I couldn't work out why. The cause was structural: snap re-snaps every programmatic scroll write, so an imperative drag and a snap container simply cannot coexist.

v2

I wrote a custom overscroll bounce for it, because native elastic scroll only exists on iOS and macOS Safari — everywhere else the deck just stopped dead at the ends. Then I deleted it. A custom rubberband runs on top of native elastic scroll rather than replacing it, and the two fighting is exactly what felt un-fluent.

v3

Stop using scroll. A hook owns a fractional index driven by drag, wheel and arrow keys, with a spring settle and a rubberband at the ends. Every card is pure geometry read off that one number. Because I now own the position, the bounce could come back.

I explored seven mechanics as throwaway grey wireframes before committing — Apple's dock, Revolut's wallet, exploded stacks, fanned tiers. Coverflow won. The six that lost are the reason I trust the one that shipped.

Overscroll resistance · the curve that shipped

Scale is the only focus cue

Blur and opacity are both gone. They made the neighbouring cards look broken rather than unfocused. Scale is mapped continuously from distance to centre, eased so most of the change happens near the middle — so cards grow into focus under your finger instead of popping when the active card changes.

The cards are the only colour

Every UI primitive draws from one 12-step neutral ramp that inverts between themes. Buttons, sliders, focus rings, the step indicator — all grey. The cards are deliberately the only source of colour in the entire app, so nothing competes with the thing you're making.

03of three

Ship it

The card is laid on a carrier sheet, folded in thirds, slid into a kraft envelope, sealed and flipped to show the address side. Then you drag it into a mail slot yourself.

Five seconds, eight beats, no skip button. The whole thing is armed by the forward arrow rather than by arriving on the step — so the finished card gets a rest beat to itself first, with one line above it: One of one.

Scrub the choreography

Everything is specified in millimetres

The card is ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1 — 85.6 × 53.98mm, 3.18mm radius — at every size it appears. Wrong card proportions are the first thing a design reviewer notices and there's no recovering from it.

So the whole sequence is drawn in real units and converted by one scale factor. The slot is the widest object, so it sets the horizontal scale. The sheet is the tallest, so it binds the vertical. No raw pixel exists inside the stage except hairlines and shadows.

The decision I locked and then broke

Before writing a line of this I wrote a full build spec with a ten-row decision table. D10: fold the snapshot PNG, never the live card — because a WebGL canvas inside a 3D transform context is a known Safari failure mode, and I'd flagged it as the highest craft risk in the project.

D10 was defensive against a risk the geometry had already removed. The card sits on the middle panel of a three-panel fold, and the middle panel never rotates — only the two outer ones do. The card also unmounts entirely at the insert beat, before anything containing it moves in 3D. The dangerous case couldn't happen.

So the sequence folds the live card. Real component, real pattern, all the way into the envelope. No capture step, no imperceptible swap to disguise. A whole dependency got deleted.

Writing the spec first is still why the geometry was solid enough to notice the contradiction. But a locked decision is only as good as the assumption under it — and mine had expired two phases earlier without anyone re-reading it.

The fidelity gap is the focus device

The carrier sheet is a deliberate low-fidelity wireframe — placeholder bars, no real copy. The card is the only fully rendered object in the frame, so even while it's being covered up it stays the hero. The wireframe stops at the sheet, because the envelope has to carry real text.

It's impossible to fail the drag

Release anywhere past halfway, or with any downward velocity, and the envelope snaps into the slot. The gesture is the ceremony, not a test. Under reduced motion it becomes a button — the terminal action of a flow can never be gesture-only.

Slowing seven springs without changing any of them

The whole sequence runs 1.25× slower than it was first tuned. That's not seven separate re-tunings — slowing a spring by k/T² and c/T holds the damping ratio exactly. The overshoot, the rock at settle, the shape of every arrival is identical. Only the pace changed.

Which matters because the envelope's flip is deliberately under-damped — it has a small rock as it settles, and that rock is the signature of the whole beat. It had to survive the tempo change untouched.

Across all three

One card, never re-mounted

The card is a single React node mounted once at the top of the app that never unmounts for the entire journey. Each step reports where it should sit via an invisible spacer, and the card springs its transform to that point.

This replaced a shared-element hand-off, which popped — the measured box was being distorted by the deck's coverflow transform, so the hand-off was reading a lie about where the card was. With one persistent node there's nothing to measure and nothing to hand off.

One object travels through the whole story. That's the craft signature of the piece, and it's also the thing that was hardest to get right.

Two bugs from that pass worth keeping: a settle flash that turned out to be two shadows at partial alpha compositing lighter than one at full — crossfading pixel-identical elements is not free, you have to swap. And clip-path on a preserve-3d element silently flattens it, which cost two commits and a revert.

Process

A playground before the product

Carousel behaviour, colour, typography, card graphics, and the whole envelope choreography — I tested all of it on separate dev routes first, each with its own control panel and a copy button handing me apply-ready values.

Nothing landed in the real build before I knew how it would behave. The ship sequence especially was built against a scrubbable timeline — you cannot tune the fourth beat of a five-second animation if you have to sit through the first three every time.

The ship sequence came out of four steps, and only the last one was slow: think out loud about what I wanted and let Claude ask me the questions; write a full build spec before any code; new branch wired into the existing scaffold with nothing else touched; then hand-tune the easing and timing until it felt right.

The spec has a section listing decisions I'd declined, recorded so neither of us would propose them again three days later. That section saved more time than any of the code.

Working with AI isn't prompting for output. It's writing the constraints down hard enough that the output has nowhere bad to go.

Responsiveness and keyboard depth were the trade for depth on the core interaction. Both are the first thing I pick back up, and neither is a hard problem — just an unspent day.

What I learnt

AI got me to ninety percent in two days. The last ten percent took the rest of the week, and it's the only part anybody feels when they use it.

Constraints are invisible work. Nobody should ever see a guardrail. They should only notice that nothing they make comes out looking bad.

Every choice I removed made the output better. Restraint did more here than range ever did — and I only know that because I built the range first and watched it not help.
